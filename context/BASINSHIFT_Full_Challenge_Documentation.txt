1. Executive summary
BASINSHIFT is a proposed international machine-learning challenge focused on forecasting river discharge and detecting
high-flow events in geographically unfamiliar catchments. The central research question is whether an ML system can learn
transferable hydrological relationships rather than relying on patterns specific to catchments observed during training.
Participants will receive approved historical hydrological observations, meteorological inputs and catchment attributes. They
will build systems that produce future discharge forecasts, probabilities of crossing high-flow thresholds and uncertainty
estimates. Evaluation will include ordinary forecasting, rare-event detection, geographic generalization, calibration,
robustness and computational efficiency.
The challenge is deliberately framed as a benchmark for generalization under environmental and geographic shift. It is not a
request to build a complete flood-warning service, estimate street-level inundation, or replace official hydrological agencies.
Recommended direction
Proceed to benchmark engineering, not immediate public launch. The concept is promising, but launch
readiness depends on dataset licensing and quality checks, aligned target/input definitions, leakage-resistant
splits, baseline reproduction, hidden-evaluator testing and an independent pilot.
Key design principles
●
 Use genuine, documented hydrological observations for evaluation.
●
 Separate catchment identities and time periods to test distinct forms of generalization.
●
 Treat discharge forecasting, high-flow exceedance and inundation mapping as different tasks.
●
 Reward calibrated uncertainty and robust performance, not only average prediction error.
●
 Make the benchmark architecture-neutral and computationally accessible.
●
 Keep hidden labels, exact test partitions and test-generation details confidential.

2. Challenge identity and scope
Working title: BASINSHIFT — The Global Flood Intelligence Challenge.
The name refers to the core technical challenge: learning across basin boundaries and operating under distribution shift. The
title is provisional and should undergo trademark, domain and event-brand checks before publication.
2.1 Challenge category
Primary category: Machine Learning / Environmental Intelligence / Hydrological Forecasting. Secondary themes include
time-series modeling, domain generalization, probabilistic prediction, transfer learning and robust ML.
2.2 Core objective
Develop a reproducible ML system that predicts river discharge at specified future horizons and estimates the probability of
high-flow and extreme high-flow threshold exceedances, including for catchments excluded from model training.
2.3 In scope
●
 Time-series prediction of river discharge.
●
 High-flow threshold exceedance probability estimation.
●
 Generalization to catchments not seen during training.
●
 Uncertainty estimation and calibration.
●
 Robustness to missing, noisy or degraded input observations.
●
 Efficient and reproducible model inference.
2.4 Out of scope
●
 Certified public flood-warning decisions.
●
 Street-level flood depth or building-level inundation prediction in the core track.
●
 Claims of causal climate attribution.
●
 Autonomous emergency response or evacuation recommendations.
●
 Replacement of official hydrological forecasts or local expert judgment.
2.5 Intended participants
The challenge is intended for university teams, independent researchers, ML engineers, data scientists and multidisciplinary
teams. The benchmark should be usable by teams with CPU-only or modest GPU resources; compute-intensive extensions
may be optional rather than mandatory.

3. Problem background and motivation
River discharge is the volume of water flowing through a river cross-section per unit time. Forecasting discharge is a key
component of hydrological monitoring and can support decisions in flood preparedness, reservoir operations, agriculture,
infrastructure management and water-resource planning.
Catchments differ in climate, topography, soil, vegetation, land use, geology, river network structure and human intervention.
A model trained on one set of basins may not transfer reliably to another. Temporal changes, rare extreme events and gaps
in observation records further complicate learning.
A benchmark that evaluates only random samples from familiar catchments can overestimate real-world generalization.
Overlapping time windows, shared catchment characteristics and temporally adjacent records may allow a model to perform
well without learning transferable relationships.
3.1 Why this matters internationally
Flood hazards cross national and administrative boundaries, while hydrological data availability and monitoring quality vary
widely. A generalizable approach could be useful in data-sparse basins, but that possibility must be demonstrated rather than
assumed. A global benchmark can expose where models transfer, where they fail and how uncertainty changes under
unfamiliar conditions.
3.2 The precise problem
Given a permitted history of hydrological and meteorological observations plus static or slowly varying catchment attributes,
estimate river discharge at one or more future forecast horizons. For each horizon, also estimate the probability that
discharge exceeds a pre-defined catchment-specific high-flow threshold.
3.3 Why the challenge is difficult
●
 Hydrological response is nonlinear and depends on interactions among rainfall, soil, terrain and catchment storage.
●
 Catchments have different response times and physical characteristics.
●
 Extreme events are relatively rare and may be underrepresented in training data.
●
 Observation records can be incomplete, noisy or inconsistent.
●
 Geographic and temporal shifts can invalidate learned correlations.
●
 Point forecasts alone do not communicate the risk of extreme outcomes.
3.4 Intended impact pathway
A successful research result may inform future decision-support systems, especially where model transfer and uncertainty
are important. Before operational use, the system would require local validation, expert oversight, reliable data feeds,
monitoring, and formal evaluation against relevant agency standards.

4. Research gap and ML justification
The competition is not based on the claim that hydrological forecasting is unsolved. Existing hydrological models, operational
forecasting systems and ML methods already provide important capabilities. The proposed opportunity is to evaluate and
improve reliability when models are transferred to unseen catchments and unusual conditions.
4.1 Research questions
1
How well can a model forecast discharge in catchments excluded from training?
2
Which static catchment attributes and dynamic forcing variables support transfer?
3
How does performance change between familiar and unfamiliar geographic regions?
4
Can probabilistic forecasts remain calibrated during high-flow events?
5
How sensitive are models to missing, noisy or delayed observations?
6
Do hybrid or physics-informed approaches improve generalization enough to justify their complexity?
4.2 Why machine learning is justified
The task involves learning nonlinear relationships across multiple input variables and catchments, handling heterogeneous
time series, transferring representations across domains and estimating conditional predictive distributions. ML offers a family
of methods suited to these tasks. However, the competition must permit statistical and physical baselines so that ML is
required to demonstrate value rather than assumed to be superior.
4.3 Research hypotheses to test

5. Official participant-facing problem statement
BASINSHIFT: Learning to Predict Extreme River Events Beyond Observed
Environments
Background
Flood-related decisions depend in part on understanding how rivers respond to rainfall and changing environmental
conditions. Yet hydrological systems differ substantially from one catchment to another. Models that perform well in familiar
locations may not maintain the same reliability in geographically unfamiliar basins or during unusual events.
The challenge
Build a machine-learning system that forecasts future river discharge and estimates the probability of
high-flow events across diverse catchments. Your solution must demonstrate generalization beyond the
catchments used for training and provide evidence about the reliability of its predictions.
Core requirements
●
 Produce river-discharge forecasts for the official forecast horizons.
●
 Estimate high-flow and extreme high-flow exceedance probabilities.
●
 Demonstrate performance on unseen catchments.
●
 Quantify uncertainty using a documented method.
●
 Handle missing or degraded observations according to the benchmark specification.
●
 Provide reproducible code, model artifacts and technical documentation.
Potential input data
The organizer-provided package may include historical discharge, meteorological observations or forcing, catchment
attributes, timestamps and data-quality indicators. The final data dictionary will specify the exact available variables, units,
missing-value conventions and permitted use.
Expected output
For each evaluated catchment and forecast origin, teams will submit predicted discharge for each official horizon, high-flow
exceedance probabilities, uncertainty information and required metadata in the published schema.
Permitted methods
Teams may use statistically justified approaches, tree-based models, neural sequence models, graph models, probabilistic
methods, transfer learning, domain adaptation, physics-informed methods or hybrid systems. No specific architecture is
mandatory. All external data, pretrained models and synthetic-data use must be declared.
Success
An exceptional solution will combine strong forecast skill with reliable high-flow detection, transfer to unseen catchments,
calibrated uncertainty, robustness and reproducible engineering. The competition does not certify a system for operational
flood warnings.
Official wording must be updated after the organizer has frozen the forecast horizons, variable list, data
policy, split definitions and submission schema.

6. Dataset and data governance strategy
6.1 Primary resource: Caravan
Caravan is a large-sample hydrology dataset designed to make data from multiple catchment datasets more accessible in a
standardized structure. The challenge proposal uses it as the primary starting point because it combines streamflow
observations, meteorological forcing and catchment attributes across many catchments.
The often-cited collection size is 6,830 catchments. This figure must be checked against the exact release, version and files
selected by the organizers. It must not be treated as a guarantee that every variable or every date is complete for every
catchment.

The benchmark's credibility depends on split design. The organizing committee should define splits at the
catchment level before generating windows, sequences or samples. Randomly splitting overlapping windows
is not sufficient for a geographic-generalization challenge.

7.2 Leakage controls
●
 Assign catchment IDs to partitions before constructing training windows.
●
 Ensure no target labels from hidden periods enter training-derived statistics.
●
 Compute catchment thresholds from an approved historical reference period only.
●
 Fit preprocessing parameters using training data only.
●
 Audit external datasets for hidden-period or hidden-location labels.
●
 Inspect public mirrors and derived products for possible reconstruction of hidden targets.
●
 Check that forecast input timestamps never include information after the forecast origin.
●
 Run duplicate and near-duplicate record checks across partitions.
7.3 Geographic split strategy
Do not rely on a single random basin split. Consider region-stratified holdouts so that test partitions include a defensible mix
of climatic and geographic conditions. Report the distribution of catchment area, aridity, elevation, climate zone and record
length across splits. The exact strategy should be selected after coverage analysis.
7.4 Temporal split strategy
For temporal testing, use chronologically later periods than the training period. Avoid random time shuffling. Ensure that the
input history available at each forecast origin is strictly prior to the target horizon. Document whether a warm-up context is
permitted for a test catchment and whether that context contains labels.
7.5 Stress-test design
Apply reproducible perturbations such as fixed rates of missing observations, short contiguous gaps, measurement noise and
delayed input availability. Publish the perturbation families and general methodology, but keep exact hidden-test seeds and
examples confidential until after the competition.
7.6 Split acceptance criteria
The benchmark is not ready until independent reviewers confirm that split assignments are reproducible, the hidden targets
cannot be trivially reconstructed, the partitions are sufficiently large for stable metrics and no accidental identity or time
leakage remains.

8. ML task and input/output contract
8.1 Task formulation
For catchment c and forecast origin t, the model receives a permitted input history X(c, ≤t) and static or slowly varying
attributes A(c). It predicts discharge q(c,t+h) at each official horizon h, plus the probability of exceeding the official threshold
T(c).
The forecast horizons should be selected after inspecting the temporal resolution, label coverage and
practical baseline difficulty. A provisional set of 1, 3 and 7 days may be explored only if the data support those
horizons; it is not a frozen requirement.

The competition should be architecture-neutral. Participants should be able to justify any method that satisfies the interface
and constraints. Organizers should provide strong, reproducible baselines to establish task difficulty and reduce the risk that
the benchmark is accidentally trivial or impossible.

9.2 Candidate participant methods
●
 Gradient-boosted decision trees using lagged forcing, discharge history and catchment attributes.
●
 Recurrent models such as LSTM or GRU for sequential dependencies.
●
 Temporal convolutional networks for multi-scale temporal patterns.
●
 Transformer-style sequence models where the data volume and compute justify them.
●
 Graph neural networks if river-network structure is reliably available and standardized.
●
 Probabilistic regression, quantile models or distributional forecasting.
●
 Domain adaptation, meta-learning or invariant representation methods.
●
 Physics-informed losses or hybrid physical/ML models, with constraints documented.
9.3 Model selection discipline
Teams should select methods based on the data regime, forecast horizon, compute budget and validation evidence. A
complex model is not inherently superior. The final report should include ablations showing which components materially
improve performance.
9.4 Compute fairness
Set an inference environment and resource budget before launch. Consider CPU inference as the baseline requirement, with
a modest optional GPU allowance if the organizer platform supports it. The precise CPU, RAM, accelerator, runtime and
artifact-size limits must be published after a pilot.

10.3 Normalization
A defensible option is to map each metric to a bounded score using fixed baseline anchors and predeclared caps. For
example, improvement over a baseline can be normalized against a fixed target range. The formula must handle negative
skill, zero denominators, degenerate cases and missing outputs. Do not choose normalization after seeing participant results.
10.4 Statistical reporting
●
 Report both macro-average across catchments and pooled observation-level results.
●
 Use catchment-level bootstrap intervals where appropriate.
●
 Report event counts and denominators with every rare-event metric.
●
 Report performance by region, catchment characteristics and event severity.
●
 Record variance across random seeds for stochastic methods.
●
 Do not claim meaningful superiority when confidence intervals overlap materially.
●
 Publish failure cases and metric limitations after the competition.

10.5 Avoiding accuracy-only ranking
A model may achieve low average error while missing rare extreme events, or may issue too many false alarms. A
probabilistic model may be better calibrated even if its point forecast is slightly less accurate. The scoring system must
preserve these distinctions.

Use a limited number of public leaderboard submissions to reduce overfitting. The number, reset policy, late-submission

seeds, private labels or implementation details that would enable direct reconstruction. Judges and

16.1 Responsible-use statement
BASINSHIFT is an experimental research benchmark. Model outputs must not be used as the sole basis for
evacuation, emergency response, infrastructure operation or public warning decisions. Any future
deployment requires local validation, hydrological expert review, appropriate data governance, operational
monitoring, fail-safe procedures and coordination with competent authorities.
16.2 Known limitations of the proposed design
●
 Caravan's global coverage does not mean uniform coverage, complete records or identical data quality.
●
 Catchment discharge is a point or basin-level hydrological variable and does not directly describe local flood depth or
exposure.
●
 High-flow percentile thresholds are statistical benchmark labels, not official hazard thresholds.
●
 Publicly available secondary datasets may have different spatial and temporal semantics.
●
 Benchmark performance does not establish causal understanding or operational readiness.
●
 Results may depend on the selected regions, periods, horizons and data versions.

GO: all critical gates pass, baseline difficulty is meaningful, the evaluator is reproducible, and independent reviewers approve

to reproduce final submissions.
17.2 Final committee recommendation
Proceed with BASINSHIFT as the leading challenge concept and begin a time-boxed feasibility and
benchmark-engineering phase. Do not advertise the challenge as a validated global flood-prediction
benchmark until the data pipeline, baseline suite, hidden evaluator and independent pilot have passed their
acceptance gates.
