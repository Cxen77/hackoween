FLOODCAST
 Predict the Surge Before It Strikes
Disaster Risk & Early Warning Systems
 Regression Challenge
24-Hour Hackathon  ·  November 2026
Overview
Every year, floods kill thousands and displace millions. The difference between a community that
evacuates in time and one that doesn't often comes down to a single number: how severe will the river
surge be?
Meteorological agencies have the data. What they lack are models that generalize — systems that can
estimate flood severity at a river station they have never seen before, using only rainfall, temperature, and
basic catchment characteristics.
That is what you are building.
Problem Statement
Given historical river discharge, rainfall, and temperature data for a set of river stations, build a
regression model that predicts the peak daily discharge (in cubic feet per second) at a station
for each day in the test period.
Formally:
For each (station_id, date) pair in test.csv, predict:
   discharge_cfs — river discharge in cubic feet per second (continuous, non-negative)
 What discharge means:
Discharge is the volume of water flowing past a river cross-section per second. Low discharge = normal
river. High discharge = flood risk. Very high discharge = dangerous flooding. The model must predict the
actual number, not a category.
Why this is hard:
   Hydrological response is nonlinear — a small increase in rainfall can cause a large surge
   Every station behaves differently based on catchment area, soil type, and slope
   Extreme flood events are rare and underrepresented in training data
   Models must work on stations not seen during training (geographic generalization)

Evaluation Criteria
Your submission is auto-scored against hidden ground-truth discharge values. The final leaderboard score
is a weighted combination of four technical metrics. There is no manual component in the auto-score.
Primary Metric — Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
RMSE = sqrt( mean( (predicted_discharge - actual_discharge)^2 ) )
Lower RMSE is better. RMSE is computed across all rows in test.csv. The
leaderboard ranks teams by RMSE ascending.
RMSE penalizes large errors heavily. A model that mostly predicts correctly but catastrophically fails on flood peaks
will score worse than a model that is consistently slightly off. This is intentional — missing a flood peak matters most.

How the final score is computed:
Each metric is normalized to a 0–100 scale using the best and worst scores seen across all submissions.
The weighted average of the four normalized scores gives the final leaderboard score (higher = better).
 RMSE_norm = 100 * (worst_RMSE - your_RMSE) / (worst_RMSE - best_RMSE)
The normalization is computed after the submission window closes using the actual range of scores. This means your
leaderboard position during the event is based on raw RMSE only. Final ranks use the weighted composite.
